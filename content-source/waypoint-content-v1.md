## Discover Waypoint

Waypoint is being developed as a small, part-time provision where young people learn through practical experiences and supportive relationships.

The programme is designed for ages 8–16. School-funded placements and home-education Discovery places are considered individually, taking account of the young person, group and available support.

## Activities with a purpose

Build the Basecamp

Plan and build a temporary base using materials such as tarpaulins and ropes. Decide what makes it useful, test ideas and improve the design. There are opportunities to measure, explain choices and share responsibility.

The Giant Chain Reaction

Connect ramps, balls, tubes and everyday materials into a sequence. Test what happens, investigate where it stops and try another solution. Listening and problem-solving matter as much as the finished result.

Survival Village

Create an imaginary settlement, plan shelters and decide how to use limited materials. The group has to agree priorities, divide responsibilities and adapt its plans.

Waypoint Game Studio

Dream up characters, game rules and levels together. An adult can use suitable digital tools to explore whether the group's ideas can become a simple game to test and improve. Young people lead the creative ideas while an adult operates the game-making tools.

These are examples from our developing activity bank, not a fixed timetable. Activities are selected to suit the group, interests, agreed individual goals where relevant, safety and conditions. Our first term will be land-based, with no paddling, water activities or climbing.

## A familiar shape to the day

Arrive and settle: a welcome, a chance to connect and an explanation of the plan.

Get involved: start a meaningful activity with a demonstration or a manageable first step.

Pause: breaks and lunch provide time to rest and reconnect.

Develop the project: continue, improve an idea or take on another experience.

Finish together: look back at something that happened and prepare for leaving.

We plan to share a simple outline in advance. The rhythm provides predictability while allowing the activities to respond to the group and conditions.

## Help that makes participation possible

A young person may understand a task more easily when they see it demonstrated. Another may need instructions one step at a time, an adult alongside them or a short pause.

We look at what is getting in the way and help them work towards joining or returning to the activity. Clear boundaries and supervision remain part of that support.

We want young people to experience achievable challenge and gradually take more responsibility.

## Learning that can be seen

Practical tasks create real opportunities to read instructions, measure materials, explain ideas, negotiate and solve problems.

We distinguish the learning opportunity from what a young person actually demonstrates. Completing a task with support and completing it independently tell us different things.

For school-funded placements, agreed individual goals help us connect real participation and learning to useful observations and next steps. Discovery focuses on enriching home education through practical, shared experiences; we explain its family-feedback arrangements before booking.

How a project becomes purposeful learning

An illustrative example — this describes a possible approach, not a real learner’s progress or a fixed programme.

Starting point: imagine a young person who enjoys making things but finds it difficult to explain an idea in a group. We first ask about their experience and the support that helps.

Individual goal: agree a useful way for them to contribute an idea, whether through speaking, sketching, showing or another suitable method.

Activity: a Giant Chain Reaction project gives the idea a purpose. The group needs to work out how to move a ball from one part of the course to the next.

Support: an adult might demonstrate a connection, break the task into smaller steps or help the young person show their design. The choice depends on what helps that individual.

Evidence: we notice what they actually contributed, what support they used and what they thought of the experience. A successful group result alone does not show what each person learned.

Next step: revisit a useful opportunity in a later session, retain necessary support and review what happens. A further step might be explaining a change, comparing two designs or applying an idea to a different project. It depends on the learner’s experience, not a fixed timetable.

We discuss useful observations with the school and family where appropriate. Something working at Waypoint is a starting point for that conversation; we do not assume it will work in every setting.

## Progress does not have to be linear

Creating an idea, trying a new skill, contributing to a group or asking for help can all be worthwhile parts of learning.

One achievement might be developing an idea, another working with someone new or finding a different way to solve a problem. We value curiosity, contribution and learning along the way, not only the finished result.

## For Schools

### Looking beyond the response

At Waypoint, a purposeful day has two aims: giving a young person a meaningful learning experience, and helping the adults around them understand what makes participation possible. We are developing a small-group, part-time alternative provision in Shoreham-by-Sea for pupils aged 8–16.

Difficulty is not always obvious. Some pupils push back; others withdraw, avoid unfamiliar challenges or appear to cope while struggling. These are responses to understand, not types of children. Through hands-on projects and supported group tasks, we aim to discover what helps a pupil begin, communicate, contribute and recover after a setback — then use that understanding to plan the next useful step with school and family.

## What a school-funded place is designed to provide

## An experience worth taking part in: planned, staff-led practical projects that offer ways to make, solve problems, collaborate and experience achievement, with appropriate support.

## A better understanding of support: observations of what the pupil can do, the conditions that help and what still presents difficulty — not judgments made from a label or the finished task alone.

## Information for the next decision: a weekly report, agreed reviews and realistic suggestions to explore in the pupil's wider education plan. One Tuesday complements that plan; it does not replace a full school timetable or guarantee a particular outcome.

## Assessing Waypoint as a provider

Headteachers and senior leaders need to decide whether Waypoint is an appropriate provider for their school before an individual pupil is considered. We welcome questions about the educational purpose, leadership, safeguarding approach, practical delivery and oversight.

Schools can discuss a provider visit and request information about leadership, safeguarding, recruitment, premises, insurance and placement arrangements. We will provide the current approved evidence available and identify outstanding requirements clearly before any placement is agreed.

Provider assessment and individual pupil suitability are separate steps. Once your school is satisfied that Waypoint may be an appropriate provider, we work with your SENCo or inclusion lead to consider a particular pupil.

## For SENCos and inclusion leads — considering a pupil

Waypoint may be worth considering when a pupil could benefit from purposeful practical learning, a smaller group and adults who take time to understand what supports participation. Some young people show their difficulties clearly; others mask them. We look at strengths, interests and the next useful step rather than deciding suitability from a behaviour label.

Our questions begin with what you already know. Where does this pupil thrive? What barriers appear at school, and what support currently helps? How much adult input is needed? What do the pupil and family say? What would make a Tuesday at Waypoint purposeful rather than simply a change of scenery? We aim to build on your existing understanding, not repeat an assessment or assume a cause from behaviour.

Before offering a place, we consider the pupil's current support and relevant plans alongside the actual group, site, adult availability, safeguarding arrangements and realistic adaptations. Dedicated one-to-one support is not included automatically. A small group or a place offered to another pupil does not establish this pupil's suitability.

Where a young person’s needs or risks exceed the support available, we will explain this before a place is agreed. We cannot assume that a small group alone will meet every need.

## What helps us plan a safe, worthwhile placement

## You do not need to start with a new questionnaire. We ask for the existing information that genuinely matters: strengths and interests; the reason for the proposed placement; current communication, sensory and learning support; relevant health or safety arrangements; views of the pupil and family; and what success would usefully look like for school. We agree how to receive sensitive documents through an appropriate route.

## An individual suitability decision considers whether the support actually needed can be delivered safely alongside the rest of the group. If essential arrangements are missing or the proposed group cannot meet the pupil's needs, we will explain this rather than accept a place and hope for the best.

## Support that is practical, specific and reviewed

If a pupil finds it hard to start, an adult might demonstrate the task, make the instructions clearer or offer one manageable first step. If frustration builds, we consider what is difficult, support an agreed supervised pause when appropriate and work towards rejoining the learning. Changing the route into a task need not mean abandoning its purpose.

We maintain clear expectations and supervised boundaries. Observations help us identify which support is useful, rather than repeatedly offering an approach that is not working.

## Goals that guide the placement

Together, we agree a small number of purposeful goals with the pupil, school and family as appropriate, drawing on existing SEN Support information, EHCP provisions and outcomes where relevant. We do not independently change an EHCP or claim to replace statutory provision. A meaningful goal should make clear what we hope the pupil will be able to attempt, communicate or understand — and the support expected to help.

For example, a pupil might work towards beginning a familiar task with fewer prompts, contributing an idea to a group or returning after a supported pause. These are illustrative goals, not a standard set for every pupil.

## Evidence your school can use

Our planned standard school-funded offer includes a weekly evidence-led report for agreed school and family recipients, subject to information-sharing restrictions. It will show the task and context, what the pupil actually did or communicated, what support was planned and provided, how they responded, what remains difficult and one or two realistic things to explore next. A supported success is worth recognising — without treating it as proof the pupil now needs less help.

The fictional extract below shows why recording the task, actual adult support and remaining uncertainty is more useful than simply saying a pupil 'had a good day'.

Where available, we include the young person's views, accurately distinguish school and parent reports from our own observations, and explain uncertainty. If an approach works at Waypoint, we can discuss how it might be tried elsewhere, but we will not assume it transfers to the classroom or recommend withdrawing support based on a good Tuesday.

We agree a named school contact, authorised report recipients, attendance and safeguarding handover arrangements, weekly reporting day and review dates before the first session. If something significant changes, the agreed contact route applies promptly rather than waiting for a weekly report. The young person can also receive a simple advance outline of activities to support predictability. We use relevant existing records and ask for clarifications, rather than making SENCos complete another long assessment from scratch.

## Arranging and reviewing a placement

The route is deliberately clear: first a provider discussion with the headteacher or senior leadership; then a SENCo or inclusion-led conversation about the individual pupil, including the young person's and family's views where appropriate; then a decision about suitability and available support; and finally written agreement on goals, staffing and adjustments, safeguarding, attendance, costs, communication and reviews before confirming a start. Approval of Waypoint as a provider does not automatically approve every pupil.

Our planned review rhythm starts with checking the starting picture and support after the first two attended sessions. Fuller placement reviews are normally planned around weeks 4, 8 and 12, with dates agreed for the individual placement. Reviews consider what has been learned, the support still needed and whether to continue, adjust the arrangements or plan the next step. If needs, risks or circumstances change, we review sooner.

Our normal preparation allowance is five working days after all essential information is received, subject to suitability, staffing, availability and safe arrangements; an earlier start may sometimes be considered individually. This is not a guaranteed start date. The How referrals work page explains the stages and information required.
